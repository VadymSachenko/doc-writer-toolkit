# Abbreviations, contractions, and capitalization

## GDSG-ABBR-001 — Introduce unfamiliar abbreviations

At first mention, write the full term followed by the abbreviation in parentheses; use
the abbreviation alone afterward. Under the Google guide, italicize both the full term
and abbreviation in the introduction.

**Pattern:** *distributed denial-of-service (DDoS)* attack

Do not expand universally familiar technical abbreviations such as AI, API, DVD, HTML,
PDF, PC, RAM, REST, URL, USB, or XML unless the audience needs it. Do not introduce an
abbreviation that appears only once or is peripheral to the topic.

## GDSG-ABBR-002 — Preserve the term’s case and grammar

- Lowercase the expanded term unless it is a proper noun or convention requires caps.
- Use no periods in acronyms or initialisms. A shortened word may retain a customary
  period.
- Do not use an abbreviation as a verb.
- Form plurals normally and do not use an apostrophe.
- Choose **a** or **an** according to how the abbreviation is pronounced.

## GDSG-ABBR-003 — Prefer words to Latin shorthand

- Use **for example** instead of **e.g.**
- Use **that is** instead of **i.e.**
- Avoid **etc.** when a specific list, **and more**, or no ending is clearer.
- Avoid chat and internet shorthand in formal documentation.

## GDSG-CAPS-001 — Use sentence case

Use sentence case for document titles, headings, captions, list items, and table text,
except for proper nouns, official product names, and literal UI labels.

After a colon, normally begin with lowercase. Capitalize after a colon only for a proper
noun, a heading, a quotation that is a complete sentence, or a notice label followed by
a complete sentence.

Do not describe casing with ambiguous informal names such as *camel case* when an exact
description or example is more useful.

## GDSG-CONTRACT-001 — Use clear common contractions

Common two-word contractions support a conversational tone. Avoid a contraction when it
is awkward, unfamiliar, easily misread, or makes a requirement ambiguous. In particular,
consider expanding negative contractions in high-stakes instructions.
